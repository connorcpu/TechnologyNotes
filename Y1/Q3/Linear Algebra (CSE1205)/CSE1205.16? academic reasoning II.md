## 1 eigenvectors and eigenvalues
- let A & B be n x n matrices. if $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of both A and B. then $\lambda$ is also an eigenvalue of AB
- statement is not true
   - $Av = \lambda v \land Bw = \lambda w$
   - for the sake of simplicity (since the counterexample also works with this assumption) we assume $\lambda v$ is an eigenvector of B
   - $ABv = (v \cdot \lambda)B = v \cdot \lambda^2$ 
   - in general $\lambda^2 \neq \lambda$
   - therefore a counterexample can be thought up where A = B where $\sigma(A) \neq 1$
   - counterexample: $A = B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$
      - $\sigma(A) = \sigma(B) = 2$
      - $\sigma(AB) = 4$
      - $2 \neq 4$

## 2 
- if $\lambda is an eigenvalue of A then it is also an eigenvalue of $A^T$
- the statement is true by direct proof
   - assume $\lambda$ is a eigenvalue of A
   - therefore $\det(A - \lambda I)$ = 0
   - since $\lambda I = (\lambda I)^T$ $det(A^T - \lambda I) = det(A^T - (\lambda I)^T)$
   - by transpose operational rules: $det(A^T - (\lambda I)^T) = det((A - \lambda I)^T)$
   - by determinant operational rules: $det((A - \lambda I)^T) = det(A - \lambda I)$
